Where most couples find us

You didn't fall out of love. You fell into logistics.

Nobody warns you that a marriage can go quiet without going wrong. The calendar fills. The conversations shrink to handovers, bills, who is collecting whom. You still care about each other. You just cannot remember the last time you were curious about each other.

Most couples arrive telling us the same thing. We don't fight. We're good parents, good housemates, good at the admin of a shared life. And underneath it, a quieter sentence: I miss you, and you're right here.

That's not a broken relationship. That's a relationship that has been surviving on logistics, and logistics can't hold desire. Something has to interrupt the pattern, in the body, not just in conversation.

So we walk. Five days in alpine country, with the therapy woven through it. What the two of you cannot say sitting across a coffee table has a way of arriving on a ridge, three hours in, side by side, with nowhere else to be.

What the five days move

Six things that shift, and stay shifted.

The cycle, named

Every couple has one loop they've been running for years. You pursue, they withdraw, you pursue harder. We name yours out loud, and once it is named it stops running you from underneath.

Repair that holds

Apologies that land, instead of apologies that end the conversation. You will practise the repair while we are standing there, so it is a skill you take home and not a moment you had on holiday.

Desire, off the script

Long relationships write a sexual script and then follow it for a decade. We look at yours honestly, including the parts nobody has said aloud, and give you permission to write a different one.

Different by design

Desire discrepancy is not a fault line, it is the most ordinary thing in a long marriage. We stop treating the gap as evidence that something is wrong and start working with it.

The trail does the talking

Walking side by side lowers the stakes of a hard sentence. There is no eye contact to manage and nowhere to storm off to. The country holds the conversation so you don't have to.

Touch before technique

We start where the body is, not where a manual says it should be. Slow, unhurried, nothing performed. For many couples this is the part they did not know they were allowed to want.

What makes this different

Therapy meets the trail, on purpose.

Most couples retreats keep you indoors. Most adventure trips skip the depth work. We put the two together, because the body remembers what the brain talks itself out of.

Emotionally Focused Therapy

The negative cycle, the attachment injuries underneath it, and the secure bond on the other side. This is the spine of the work.

Sex therapy

Desire discrepancy, sensate focus, and the sexual script a long marriage writes without ever discussing it. Frank, warm, never clinical.

Adventure

Walking, hiking, wild places. Effort shared in the open air does something to a couple that no amount of sitting on a sofa achieves.

Tarisha & Mark, Wanaka
We are not neutral about this. We think the second half of a marriage can be the better half.

Tarisha is a couples and sex therapist working in Auckland and online, trained in Emotionally Focused Therapy, and the host of the podcast A Radiant Woman. She has spent years in the room with couples who love each other and cannot reach each other.

Mark brings the mountains. He plans the routes, reads the weather, and makes sure that five days in alpine country feels like an adventure rather than an endurance test. Between them, the trail and the therapy stay in the same conversation.

They live in Auckland. They run the retreats in Wanaka and Queenstown, where the light in the evening does half the work for them.
