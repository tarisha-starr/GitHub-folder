# Wild Sky Coffee — Offer, Bundles and Email

Three levers, in order of how much money they move: **raise the average order**
(bundles), **fix the offer** (the open-ended 20%), **build the list asset** (a
welcome sequence behind the signup form you already have).

---

## 1. The offer problem

Right now: a permanent bar on every page saying "Sign up for our 20% off launch
special". No end date, no reason, on product pages where someone is mid-purchase.

Three things that does to you:

1. **It interrupts buyers.** A visitor ready to add a $65 press to her cart is
   told to go and get a discount first. Some of them leave to find the code and
   don't come back.
2. **It trains people to wait.** A launch special with no end date isn't a
   launch special, it's your price. The next time you email them at full price,
   nothing happens.
3. **It's the weakest thing you could lead with.** Your actual lead is free NZ
   shipping, titanium, and two people who test the gear on their own trips. 20%
   off is what a brand says when it has nothing else.

**Do this instead**

| | |
|---|---|
| Announcement bar | `Free shipping across New Zealand · Designed and tested in the New Zealand backcountry` |
| Pop-up (20s or 40% scroll, once per visitor) | `20% off your first order` — with an end date |
| Footer signup | Same offer, always visible, no interruption |
| After the launch window ends | Switch the evergreen signup offer to **10% off your first order**, and put the spare margin into a free Trail Spork on orders over $120 — a gift reads as generous, a discount reads as a price cut |

Give the launch offer a date and honour it. An open-ended "launch special" that
never ends is the kind of claim the Fair Trading Act 1986 treats as misleading,
and an expiry you actually keep makes the next one work.

## 2. Bundles — the fastest way to lift average order value

Your best customer (Sarah, avatar 2) does not want to assemble a kit out of ten
loose objects. She wants someone to tell her what the setup is. Right now nothing
on the site does that, so she buys one cup instead of four items.

Price each kit to save roughly 10% against buying separately. That is enough to
feel like the sensible choice and not enough to devalue the gear.

### The Trail Starter — $49 *(saves $6)*
Summit Mug · Wild Coffee Scooper · Trail Spork
> Everything you need to make a decent coffee on the trail, and not a gram more.
> 45g mug, 15g spork, a scoop that measures your dose for you.

Separately: $55. **The gift-under-$50 hero.**

### The Summit Kit — $119 *(saves $15)*
Alpine Pot · Summit Mug · Wild Coffee Scooper · Trail Spork
> Boil, brew, eat. The complete ultralight setup for multi-day trips, in titanium,
> weighed and listed so you know exactly what you're carrying.

Separately: $134. **Aimed squarely at Nic.** Publish the measured total weight —
this kit lives or dies on that number.

### The Hut Kit — $159 *(saves $20)*
Traverse Coffee Press · Backcountry Bean Vault · Wild Coffee Scooper · two Camp Cups
> Coffee for two in the morning, 750ml at a time. The press, the beans kept safe
> from the critters, and two 500ml cups for a proper start.

Separately: $179.

### The Slow Morning Kit — $239 *(saves $28)*
Brew Table · Traverse Coffee Press · two Kea Mountain Cups
> For the mornings when nobody needs to be anywhere. A steady table, a press that
> makes enough for two, and two double-wall cups that hold the warmth while you
> sit there a bit longer than you planned.

Separately: $267. **Your flagship.** This is the photograph that sells the brand.

**Before publishing any of these:** weigh each kit on a kitchen scale, and check
what actually nests inside the Alpine Pot. Then publish the measured numbers. "It
all packs inside the pot" is one of the most persuasive lines an ultralight brand
can write — and one of the worst to get wrong in front of people who will check.

**How to build them in Shopify:** each kit is its own product with its own
photography, not a discount-code trick. Inventory gets messier, the page converts
much better, and it earns its own search listing.

## 3. Gift season — the deadline you're already inside

It is mid-September. Christmas is the biggest thing that will happen to this
store this year, and the range is shaped for it: $14 to $168, with obvious price
bands and a story that photographs well.

**By 31 October**
- **Gifts** in the main navigation, with three bands: under $30 · around $60 ·
  the full kit. The `gift-under-30` / `gift-under-60` tags from the SEO pack make
  this collection build itself.
- A gift note field at checkout.
- The "A present for someone who loves getting outside" post published, so it has
  time to get indexed.

**By 20 November**
- `Order by [date] for Christmas delivery` in the announcement bar, once NZ Post
  publish their cut-offs. Hard dates for both NZ and Australia — the Australian
  date is weeks earlier and people always forget.
- Email the list: the gift guide, by price band.

**December**
- Two emails: one mid-month gift reminder, one "last orders" on the cut-off date.
- After the cut-off, switch the bar to "Orders now shipping in January" and keep
  selling to the people buying for themselves.

## 4. Email — the only asset here that compounds

You are collecting addresses and sending nothing. Every address sitting in that
list is cooling off.

Shopify Email's free monthly allowance will cover a list this size to start with,
and it does automations, so there's no reason to pay for anything yet.

### Welcome sequence — five emails, written once

Each one does a job beyond "buy something": it answers the question that stops
people buying.

**1. Sent immediately — "Here's your 20%, and here's who we are"**
> Subject: `Your 20% off, and a coffee in a strange place`
>
> Code, expiry date, then three short paragraphs: we're Mark and Tarisha, we're
> in Auckland, we make the coffee gear we use ourselves. One photo from a trip.
> One link: the Coffee Gear collection.

**2. Day 2 — the question everyone asks**
> Subject: `Which cup? Here's how to pick`
>
> The three-cup comparison table. 45g, 500ml, double-wall — what each one is for
> and when the cheaper one is the right answer. Saying that out loud is what makes
> the rest of the email believable. Tag whoever clicks each cup; you have just
> segmented your list by what they want.

**3. Day 4 — teach something**
> Subject: `The four minutes that fix camp coffee`
>
> The brew method: one scoop per 250ml, coarse grind, four minutes, press slowly.
> Links to the brew guide, the press and the scooper. No discount mentioned.

**4. Day 6 — show the whole setup**
> Subject: `What's in our pack (every gram of it)`
>
> The weighed two-day kit, item by item, with the total. Links to the Summit Kit
> and the Slow Morning Kit. This is your highest-revenue email; it is also just
> a genuinely interesting thing to read.

**5. Day 8 — the code expires**
> Subject: `Your 20% runs out tomorrow`
>
> Short. The code, the expiry, three bestsellers, free NZ shipping, 30-day
> returns. One line of proof from a customer once you have reviews.

Sign-off on all five: **Mark and Tarisha** — this brand's voice is the two of you,
and the warm-hug sign-off belongs to your other business.

### Three automations that matter more than any campaign

1. **Abandoned cart** — three emails at 1 hour, 24 hours, 72 hours. The first one
   is not a discount, it's reassurance: free NZ shipping, 30-day returns,
   dispatched from Auckland. Only the third mentions a code, if at all.
2. **Post-purchase review request** — 14 days after delivery, which is long
   enough for them to have taken it somewhere. Ask for two sentences and a photo
   from a trip. This is how you solve the zero-reviews problem in
   `02-site-audit.md`, and it costs nothing.
3. **Post-purchase cross-sell** — 30 days after a cup, show the press. After a
   press, show the grinder. Your range is naturally a collection; let people
   collect it.

### Ongoing newsletter — twice a month, not more

You promised twice a month in the signup copy, so keep to it. A trip with a
route, the gear that was in the pack, one brew note. Half of it already exists in
your blog and your Instagram. Sending the same story to a list you own is worth
more than posting it to an algorithm you don't.

## 5. What success looks like in 90 days

| Metric | Where it is now | Realistic target |
|---|---|---|
| Product reviews | 0 | 15+ with photos |
| Average order value | ~$50 on a single-item order | Up 25–40% once kits are live |
| Non-brand search clicks | Near zero (no title tags, no Search Console) | A baseline worth measuring against |
| Email list with a sequence behind it | Collecting, sending nothing | Five-email welcome running on autopilot |
| Gift-season readiness | Nothing on the site speaks to a gift buyer | Gifts nav, price bands and cut-off dates live by 20 November |

The honest summary: you have a brand with a voice, a range that makes sense and
two founders people will like. What you don't have is a site that answers
*which one, will it fit, can I trust you, what does delivery cost* — and a list
you actually talk to. None of that needs a designer. It needs an afternoon in
Shopify admin and the copy in these files.
