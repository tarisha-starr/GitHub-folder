# Radiant Women's Circle — Master Your Emotions

**A modular 90 to 120 minute session (runs standalone, or as an evening in the circle programme)**
**In the voice of the "Become a Sexually Empowered Radiant Woman" course**

> Last time we found our needs and learned to speak them. Tonight we go to what gets in
> the way: our emotions, and how to be with them without numbing them and without being
> swept away.

The whole session rests on one idea, and we start there:

> **Lack of safety is the problem, not the emotions.**

When we feel safe, we can feel almost anything and stay standing. When we don't, even a
small feeling floods us, or we bolt straight into our old patterns to cope. So we don't
start by trying to manage the emotions. We start by building safety. From safety, we
expand.

You'll leave with three tools you can use for life: **safety first**, **self-compassion**,
and **tapping (EFT)**, plus a way to ride the big waves like grief without going under.

This session is not about food. It's about what's underneath the reaching, whatever your
reaching looks like, food, wine, the phone, busyness, shopping, a tidy house, other
people's problems. The habit is different. The hole it's trying to fill is the same.

## Part 1 — Arrive & soften (0:00–0:10)

Welcome them, light the candle, read the circle agreements.

> Let your eyes close. Feel your feet on the floor. Soften your jaw. Drop your shoulders.
> Unclench your belly. Three slow breaths, all the way down.
>
> Tonight we're not going to run from our feelings, and we're not going to drown in them.
> We're going to learn to be with them, safely. So just for now, let whatever you're
> feeling be here. You're allowed to feel it all in this room.

One-word go-round: "How are you arriving tonight?"

---

## Part 2 — Start with safety (0:10–0:25)

This is the foundation of everything else tonight. Teach it slowly, in your own voice:

> Here's what I've come to understand. Lack of safety is the problem, not the emotions.
> Emotions aren't dangerous. Being flooded is. And we get flooded when we try to feel big
> things without a felt sense of safety underneath us.
>
> So we start with safety. And we only expand, feel more, open more, go deeper, when we're
> not overwhelmed, and when we're not sliding into our old patterns to cope. The moment
> overwhelm shows up, or you notice yourself reaching for the numbing thing, that's not
> failure. That's information. It means: come back to safety first. Then expand again.

Then give them your definition of safety. This is the heart of it:

> What is safety, really? For me it's this:
>
> - **I'm connected to myself.** I can feel my own body, my own yes and no.
> - **I feel what's good for me.** I notice what I actually want and don't want.
> - **I test it out.** I take one small step and see what happens.
> - **I read the response.** I notice how the other person, or the situation, responds.
> - **Then I choose.** I step forward, or I step away.
>
> When I move like that, connected, checking, choosing, there's no overwhelm. Because I'm
> never abandoning myself to cope. I stay with me the whole way.

Land the reframe:

> So tonight, and this week, the question isn't "how do I control this feeling?" It's "am I
> safe enough right now to feel it? And if not, how do I bring myself back to safety
> first?" Everything else we do tonight, self-compassion, tapping, riding the waves,
> stands on this ground.

**A quick embodied taste (optional, 2 min):** Have them place a hand on the heart, feel the
chair holding them, and silently run the checklist, "Right now, am I connected to myself?
What feels good for me in this moment? I get to choose." Just to feel what safety feels
like in the body.

## Part 3 — The stress–emotion loop (0:25–0:38)

> Stress causes unpleasant emotions, and emotions cause stress. It's a loop. And most of us
> were never taught what to do with a difficult feeling, so we learned to numb it instead.
>
> A lot of us use something outside ourselves to provide safety, support, connection and
> love. When we feel bored, lonely, sad, frustrated, guilty or angry, we reach for comfort.
> For some of us that's food. For others, wine, the phone, shopping, frantic busyness,
> cleaning, or getting lost in everyone else's problems. Whatever it is, we're trying to
> fill an emotional hole.
>
> And it can't be filled from the outside. When you feed physical hunger, your body tells
> you when you're full, it makes a hormone, leptin, just for that. But there's no signal
> that says, "It's enough, your emotional need is met, you can stop now." So the phone, the
> wine, the cake becomes an instant feel-better fix. And then it backfires, because we end
> up feeling guilty, and numbing to feel better doesn't actually make us feel better.
>
> The feeling that drove us to reach is still there. It still wants to be heard. One scroll
> didn't work, so next time it's an hour. One glass becomes two. Inside, we build a pressure
> cooker, adding more and more to the top of the emotional pile. Until one day it isn't big
> enough, and it explodes, a breakup, an illness, a loss, some unexpected upheaval that
> finally lets the pressure out with tears and pain.

Notice this is the pattern we talked about in Part 2. Numbing is what we do when we don't
feel safe enough to feel. It's not a character flaw. It's a nervous system looking for
safety in the only place it knows.

## Part 4 — Reflection (0:38–0:43)

> Do you use something to numb your feelings? Food, wine, the phone, busyness, shopping,
> perfectionism? Take a minute. No judgement. Just notice, honestly, what you reach for
> when a feeling gets too big.

Soft music. **One minute of silence.** Then: "Okay, completing this reflection." Invite one
or two to name it aloud, or keep it in the journal.

## Part 5 — The reframe (0:43–0:50)

> Here's the good news. As Dr Linda Bacon says, if you're an emotional eater, you don't have
> a problem with food. And I'd say it wider: if you numb, you don't have a problem with food,
> or wine, or your phone. You have a problem with taking care of yourself and fulfilling your
> needs, and often, with not feeling safe enough to feel.
>
> Isn't that good news? Because last session, you committed to meeting your needs. The
> numbing falls away by itself as you learn to fulfil those needs and build your own safety.
> The reaching was never the real problem. It was the messenger.
>
> Are you with me?

## Part 6 — Self-compassion practice (0:50–1:03)

> The practice of self-compassion helps us stop numbing, reduces stress, and helps us feel
> content. And it's one of the fastest ways to bring yourself back to safety. Let's do it
> now, together.

> Put both hands on your heart. Feel the warmth. Ask yourself, like you'd ask a dear friend,
> "How do you feel? What's going on for you?"
>
> Listen. Then say, "I hear you feel..." and name it. Sad. Tired. Frightened. Angry.
> Whatever's true.
>
> If it's a hard feeling, say, "I see this is difficult for you. I am here."
>
> Nothing to change. Nothing to deny. Just listening and being there for yourself, exactly as
> you would for a very good friend.

Sit in it a minute or two. Then:

> Next time you reach to numb, try this first. Hands on heart. "How do you feel? What's going
> on for you?" Listen, allow, validate. And I know, when we're activated, this is easier said
> than done. Which is why I want to show you how to be with the really big waves, and then
> give you one more tool.

## Part 7 — Riding the wave: grief and overwhelm without going under (1:03–1:20)

Some feelings aren't small things to be curious about. Sometimes it's a wave that could
knock you flat. Grief. Shock. The pain when someone you love dies. Self-compassion and
safety still hold here, they matter more than ever, but the first task with an overwhelming
emotion isn't to process it. It's to stay standing while it moves through you.

> Mastering emotions has never meant shutting them out, ignoring them, or numbing them to
> cope. And it doesn't mean drowning either. There's a third way, right in the middle:
> feeling into them without letting them take you over. Here's how.

**Grief comes in waves.** You don't have to feel all of it at once. A wave arrives, it
crests, and if you don't fight it or feed it, it passes, for now. Then it comes again.
That's not you failing to cope. That's grief doing exactly what grief does.

**Feel a little, then come back. (This is self-regulation.)** Let yourself go into the
feeling for a breath or two, really feel it, then deliberately come back to safety, your
feet on the floor, the chair holding you, a slow out-breath, a hand on your heart. When
you're steady, dip back in. A little in, a little out. You're teaching your body it can
touch the pain without being swept away. This is the opposite of numbing, and the opposite
of drowning. It's titrating.

**Keep one foot in the present.** While the wave moves, keep a small part of your attention
here in the room. Name five things you can see. Feel your feet. Say quietly, "Right now, in
this moment, I am here, and I am safe." That's how you feel the grief without disappearing
into it.

**Honour the body that's still bracing.** Here's the part almost no one names. You can say
the words, make your peace, forgive, diffuse the resentment, and your body can still be
braced. The mind makes peace in sentences. The body lets go in a different language,
through breath, through tears, through shaking, through movement, through touch. Making
peace in your head doesn't automatically unclench your body. So let the body finish what
the mind started. Let the tears come, they're a release, not a breakdown. Sigh out loud.
Move. Put a warm hand where it's braced and breathe into it. Tap.

**Then soothe.** After a wave, be tender. Hands on heart. "I see this is so hard. I am
here." Warmth, water, rest. You just did something brave.

> And please hear this. Grief isn't a problem to solve or master. It's love with nowhere to
> go, and it takes the time it takes. These tools aren't here to rush you through it.
> They're here so you can stay with it, in doses you can bear, without shutting down and
> without going under.

*Facilitator note: acute grief and trauma can need one-to-one support, not just a group
practice. Name that gently. And if you're leading this while grieving yourself, you're
allowed to be real in the room, that's not weakness, it's the teaching.*

## Part 8 — EFT: what it is and why it works (1:20–1:38)

> I found another way to connect with my emotions and give them space, and I find it so much
> easier and more direct. It's called the Emotional Freedom Technique. EFT, or tapping. I'm a
> certified EFT practitioner, and I'll give you a quick version tonight so you can use it in
> your life straight away.
>
> Use EFT any time a feeling is too big, or you notice the urge to numb. It helps you uncover
> what's underneath and actually settle it. It brings you back to safety in your body. EFT was
> part of how I healed my fear of public speaking. It helps me through relationship dramas. It
> helped me feel more confident in my work. It makes me a more patient mum, when my patience
> runs out with my kids, I tap, the charge dissolves, and then I can look past their behaviour
> and see what they actually need. Nika's 8 and Rimu's 4, so I get plenty of practice.

**The science, simply:**

> EFT was developed by Dr Callahan, then simplified by Gary Craig. It works with the body's
> meridian points, the same channels used in acupuncture, but with touch instead of needles.
> The sequence taps all the major endpoints at once.
>
> Every time you feel fear, upset, anger or shame, your brain goes on alert, fight, flight,
> freeze or befriend. Tapping works directly on your limbic system, the amygdala and
> hippocampus. The amygdala is your internal smoke detector. It fires at any sign of danger
> based on old experience. If a chicken frightened you as a child, it might still sound the
> alarm at a photo of a chicken. It doesn't know you're grown now, or that a picture can't
> peck. Or maybe as a girl your sister got more attention for being pretty, and to a child,
> attention means safety and survival, so now any reminder of "not pretty enough" can trip a
> stress response.
>
> Stimulating these acupoints calms the amygdala, shown on MRI and PET scans. It relaxes the
> fight-or-flight reaction so you can shift into your prefrontal cortex, your logical brain.
> From there you see the wider picture and choose your response instead of being driven by the
> feeling. Tapping also helps the hippocampus update, the part that checks old threats against
> now: "That's a chicken, not a threat. I had a bad night, I'm a bit puffy, and I'm safe. I'm
> loved. Not looking pretty doesn't make me worthless. I'm worthy no matter how I look."

**The research and the "negatives" question:**

> The clinical psychologist David Feinstein, who's reviewed research from more than a dozen
> countries, says tapping produces results that are "unusually rapid, effective, and lasting."
>
> Some worry that tapping on the negative attracts more negative. It doesn't. With tapping we
> take our dirty laundry out and wash it, instead of stuffing it in the wardrobe. We name the
> issue, we clear it, and then naturally we move into new ideas, inspiration and reframes.
> Every time you feel stress, you can clear it through tapping, the way you clean your house,
> so it doesn't build up and pollute your health and happiness.

## Part 9 — EFT: the practice (1:38–1:55)

**1. Choose your issue and be specific.**
> Think of something bothering you now, any feeling or situation. The more specific, the
> better. Not "I feel anxious," but "I feel anxious about the call tomorrow, and it means I'll
> be judged." Ask why, what really happened, or where you feel it in your body. This is your
> reminder phrase.

**2. Rate it 1 to 10.** How strong is the feeling right now?

**3. Setup statement.**
> "Even though I feel [this], I'm still willing to love and accept myself." The setup helps
> you accept yourself and show self-compassion. It relaxes you so you can clear the issue.

**4. Tap the karate chop point** (side of the hand) and say your setup statement out loud,
three times.

**5. Tap through all the points**, saying your reminder phrase out loud, five to seven times.

**6. Breathe and re-rate.** Deep breath, bring the issue back, rate it again. Repeat rounds
until it drops. Keep sipping water, water creates flow. Any hand, any speed that feels good.

**The tapping points** (show on camera): top of the head, eyebrow, side of the eye, under the
eye, under the nose, chin, collarbone, under the arm (nipple level), wrist.

> Sometimes you start on one issue and another surfaces. That's good, that's how we go deeper
> and find what's really going on.

**Live demo (Zoom):**
> I need a volunteer. Type "yes" in the chat if you'd like me to help you tap through one of
> your issues, a feeling, a confidence wobble, a hard situation, and I'll call on you.

Work one real round with a volunteer, down to a lower number. Let the room feel it work.

> If you want to go further, I recommend The Tapping Solution by Nick Ortner.

## Part 10 — Take-home & close (1:55–2:05)

> This week, three moves. When a feeling rises, or you catch yourself reaching to numb:
>
> 1. **Safety first.** Am I connected to myself? What's good for me right now? I get to choose.
> 2. **Self-compassion.** Hands on heart. "How do you feel? What's going on for you?" Listen.
> 3. **Tap** when it's big. Wash the laundry. And with the waves, feel a little, come back to
>    safety, feel a little more.
>
> You don't master your emotions by controlling them. You master them by feeling safe enough
> to finally listen to them.

Closing round, one line each: "One feeling I'm going to stop numbing and start listening to
is..." Circle answers, warm: "We hear you." Blow out the candle together.

## Facilitator notes

- **Safety is the whole frame.** Keep returning to it. Whenever the room gets activated, the
  move is always back to safety first, not push harder. Model that.
- **Do the practices, don't just describe them.** The safety taste, the self-compassion
  minute, a real tapping round, those are the session. The teaching only sets them up.
- **Waves bring tears.** That's the laundry coming out to be washed. Let the room hold it,
  keep tissues close, don't rush to soothe.
- **Off willpower, onto care.** Numbing is an unmet-need and unmet-safety signal, not a
  failure of discipline. Bring shame back to compassion every time.
- **Scope.** EFT and these practices are self-help for everyday stress and grief support, not
  a treatment for trauma. If something big surfaces, suggest one-to-one support.
