# Facebook Growth Plan

What came back when the seven prompts in `content/facebook-growth-prompts.md`
were run against this niche. Written 23 August 2026.

Everything here is a draft for Tarisha to cut, not finished copy to schedule.
The voice rules in `content/style_guide.md` were followed throughout, so no em

## 1. The niche and the audience

Five interests, five angles, ranked by how fast a page could actually grow on
each. The honest answer is at the bottom of this section and it isn't the
obvious one.

### Angle A. The marriage that isn't fighting

**The exact angle:** not "relationships", not "marriage advice". Loneliness
inside a long marriage that looks fine from the outside. The woman who can't
point to anything wrong, which is exactly why she thinks the problem is her.

**Audience:** women 45 to 65, together ten years or more, kids mostly grown or
nearly. Not in crisis, not separating. They read relationship content privately
and almost never comment under their own name.

**Why Facebook specifically:** this is the one audience Facebook still owns.
They're already there daily, they were there before Instagram existed, and
they're in the private groups where this exact sentence gets typed at midnight.

**5 topics you could post on daily:**
1. The absence of conflict isn't presence
2. Roommate phase, and how a couple slides into it without deciding to
3. What "we're fine" actually means when she says it
4. The conversations couples avoid for years
5. What the first honest conversation actually sounds like

**Competitiveness:** medium. Crowded with generic marriage advice pages, thin
on anyone naming this specific version of it.

### Angle B. Desire after 40

**The exact angle:** low desire as a signal rather than a symptom. Not how to
want more sex, but why wanting stopped, and what it's been protecting.

**Audience:** women 45 to 60, mostly partnered, some newly single. Higher
intent than angle A because they're actively searching for this already.

**Why Facebook:** the search terms in `automation/outlier_queries.json` are
what this audience types. "I don't want sex with my husband." "Why am I not
attracted to my husband anymore." They type those in private. Facebook is where
the answers get shared into groups.

**5 topics:**
1. Responsive desire, so she stops waiting to be in the mood first
2. Why pressure kills wanting faster than anything else
3. What "I've lost my libido" usually turns out to mean
4. Desire and resentment, the connection nobody names
5. Wanting to want it, which is its own separate grief

**Competitiveness:** high, and there's a second problem. Facebook throttles
distribution on anything reading as sexual content. Post after post about
libido and reach quietly dies without a strike or a notification. The workaround
is the vocabulary: closeness, wanting, reaching for each other, being met.
Same content, distribution intact.

### Angle C. Perimenopause and desire

**The exact angle:** the bit the menopause conversation skips. Everyone covers
hot flushes, sleep and HRT. Almost nobody covers what happens to wanting, and
to a marriage, while it's going on.

**Audience:** women 44 to 56, deep in symptoms, already following three
menopause accounts.

**Why Facebook:** menopause groups on Facebook are enormous and unusually
active.

**5 topics:**
1. It isn't only hormones, and being told it is keeps her stuck
2. Vaginal dryness, which she'll never say out loud but will search
3. What to say to a husband who's taking it personally
4. Why the fix she was handed didn't work
5. Grieving the body she had, without deciding it's over

**Competitiveness:** highest of the five, and be honest about why. This space
has doctors, femtech companies and supplement brands with budgets. About half
of women in perimenopause report a drop in libido, so the audience is real and
large, but a new page with zero followers will not out publish Gennev or
Bonafide. Use it as a topic inside another angle. Don't build the page on it.

### Angle D. The body has been talking

**The exact angle:** somatic work translated into plain language. The tension,
the fatigue, the shutdown, read as messages instead of malfunctions.

**Audience:** women 40 to 65, already interested in yoga, breathwork or
therapy. Warmest audience of the five, smallest.

**Why Facebook:** honestly, weaker here than the others. This performs better
on Instagram and in video.

**5 topics:**
1. Ask her how she's doing, darling
2. The override, and what it costs
3. Shutdown as protection
4. What your shoulders have been carrying
5. One minute of actually being in your body

**Competitiveness:** low, because demand is low too. Low competition and low
demand isn't a gap, it's a quiet room.

### Angle E. The cost of being the one who holds everything

**The exact angle:** the invisible load, aimed at the woman who's admired for
carrying it.

**Audience:** women 40 to 60. Broadest reach of anything here.

**Why Facebook:** this is the most shareable content on the list by a distance.
Women tag each other under it. Reach comes fast.

**5 topics:**
1. When did you stop choosing yourself
2. The hidden cost of being the strong one
3. Competence as a trap
4. Why she can't relax even when there's nothing to do
5. Resentment as information

**Competitiveness:** very high, and there's a catch worth naming. This angle
grows a page fastest and converts worst. It pulls a huge audience who want to
be seen, not an audience who want couples therapy. A page built here gets big
and sells nothing.

### The ranking, and what to actually do

By raw growth speed: E, then C, then A, then B, then D.

By growth that turns into clients: **A, then B**, and it isn't close.

Start with **A, the marriage that isn't fighting.** It's the only one of the
five where the audience Facebook still has, the thing Tarisha is genuinely
expert in, and the thing people actually pay for all point the same direction.
E grows faster and wastes the growth. C is a fight against funded competitors.

Then use B as the deeper layer once people trust the page, and pull single
posts from C and E as guest topics. D stays on Instagram.

One thing that would make this page different from everyone else in the space:
every post comes out of the room. Not "5 tips for better communication" but "a
couple sat in my office on Tuesday and said this". Nobody competing on angle A
has that, because most of them aren't therapists.

Pick it. Commit. Don't switch for 90 days.

## 2. The page setup

Three options for everything, as the prompt asks.

### Five page name options

1. **Tarisha Starr, Sex and Relationship Therapist.** Searchable, credible,
   says what it is. Boring in the way a doctor's shingle is boring, which is
   fine. Passes the coffee test because it's just her name and her job.
2. **The Love Adventure.** The brand and the domain. Lovely, and a stranger
   scrolling has no idea what it's for. Only works with a bio doing the heavy
   lifting underneath.
3. **Love and Desire After 40.** Plain, searchable, says the audience and the
   subject in five words. Strongest of the five on discovery.
4. **Close Again.** Short, memorable, and it's what she actually wants. A woman
   would say this out loud.
5. **The Lonely Marriage.** Highest scroll stop of any name here, and it names
   the problem exactly. The risk is that following it is a public admission,
   and this audience won't do that.

**Recommendation:** name the page `Love and Desire After 40` and put
`Tarisha Starr, Sex and Relationship Therapist` in the first line of the bio.
Discovery from the name, credibility from the bio. Keep The Love Adventure for
the website, where it's already doing its job.

   avoid.

Option 1 is the one. It's her strongest line, it names the reader's exact

### Profile photo

Don't generate this one. A therapist's face is the credential, and a generated
portrait is the fastest way to lose a visitor who can't say why she left. Three
directions for a real shoot:

1. **Close, warm, eye contact.** Head and shoulders, filling the frame, looking
   straight down the lens. Soft window light from one side. She looks like
   someone you could tell the truth to. This is the one.
2. **Seated, in the room.** Three quarter, in the chair she actually works in,
   slight lean forward. Reads as "she does this for a living".
3. **Outside, natural light.** Warmer and less clinical, but weaker at
   thumbnail size, where a Facebook profile photo lives.

Whatever's picked, it has to survive being 40 pixels wide. That means her face
fills the frame, high contrast between her and the background, no busy setting.

### Cover image, 3 options

1. **Two empty chairs, side by side, warm light.** Says couples, says the room,

2. **Her, cropped wide, with the one line bio set over the left third.** Best
   conversion, needs redoing whenever the bio changes.
3. **A texture in brand colours, with a single line of type.** "You can love
   him and still feel lonely." Simple, cheap, works.

## 4. One idea, ten posts

Idea taken through prompt 4: **you can love him and still feel lonely.**

1. **Educational.** "Love and closeness aren't the same thing." Two different
   needs, two different repairs, and why treating them as one keeps couples
   stuck for years.
2. **Opinion.** "Date night isn't the fix and I'll say it." Three hours of
   being polite in a restaurant isn't closeness. Say what actually is.
3. **List.** "Four things lonely marriages have in common." Logistics have
   replaced conversation. Touch has one meaning. Nobody's asked a real question
   in a year. You've both decided not to mention it.
4. **Story from the room.** A couple, married 22 years, who told me they never
   fight. She cried in the first session and couldn't say why. He had no idea.
5. **Mistake to avoid.** "The kindest thing you can do is stop pretending
   you're fine." Protecting him from the truth is what's keeping the distance
   in place.
6. **Quick tip.** The one question that changes a first session: what did you
   used to talk about?
7. **Discussion.** "When did you last surprise each other?" Genuine question,
   posted as one line, no advice underneath.
8. **Myth.** "If we don't fight, we're fine." The most expensive belief in
   long term relationships.
9. **His side.** What men actually say in the room when their wife stops
   reaching for them. Usually: I thought she just didn't want me anymore.
10. **Hope.** The couple who fixed it in six weeks, and the unglamorous thing
    that did it. Twenty minutes, three nights a week, phones in another room.

None of them open the same way. Four are shareable to a friend, three are
answerable in the comments, three are the ones she'll read privately and say
nothing.

12. Twenty two years. No fights. No touching either.
13. What if the quiet isn't peace?
14. You keep waiting for something to go wrong so you can name it.
15. Kind, considerate, and completely alone.
16. The last real conversation you had was about the dishwasher.

### The five strongest

**1. The absence of conflict isn't presence.** (hook 2) It's the whole post in
five words, it contradicts the thing she's been told her entire marriage, and
it's already her line from `content/formula.md`. She has to keep reading to
find out what presence would be instead.

**2. The couples I worry about most are the ones who never argue.** (hook 8)
Opens from inside the room, so it carries authority in the first six words. It
also makes her want to know whether she's one of them, which is the strongest
kind of pull there is.

**3. There's nothing wrong. That's the problem.** (hook 11) Two fragments, a
full stop where she expects a comma. It's the exact sentence she's been circling
without saying, and the contradiction is unresolved, so she reads on to resolve
it.

**4. You didn't drift apart. You got organised.** (hook 20) Takes the phrase
she'd use, drift apart, and swaps it for something more accurate and more
uncomfortable. Drifting sounds like weather. Organised sounds like a choice,
which means it can be unmade.

**5. Fighting means someone still wants something.** (hook 18) Genuinely new
idea, delivered flat. It reframes the thing she's most relieved about as the
thing she's lost, and that's a shareable thought.

Hooks 9, 12 and 16 are strong lines that don't lead. Keep them for body copy.

## 7. Scaling the winners

**This one couldn't be run properly, and here's why.**

Prompt 7 needs a real post with real views, reactions, comments and shares.
There's nothing in this repo carrying performance data. `content/posts.json`
has 28 written posts with no metrics attached, and `content/outliers.csv` is
empty apart from its header row. Anything produced from that would be pattern
matching dressed up as analysis, which is exactly what the prompt is meant to
prevent.

So this section is the method plus one worked example off a hypothesis rather
than data. It's clearly marked as such.

### To run it for real

1. Open the page, Professional dashboard, then Content.
2. Sort by reach for the last 90 days.
3. Take the top post. Copy the full text and the four numbers.
4. Paste it into prompt 7 in `content/facebook-growth-prompts.md`.
5. Do it again with the post that got the most comments, which is usually a
   different post. Reach and comments reward different things.

### What to record each time

Add these to a sheet as you go, because the pattern only appears after eight or
ten posts:

`date, hook, hook pattern, topic, format, image, views, reactions, comments, shares, saves, comment keyword used, DMs it produced`

The last column is the one that matters and the one everyone forgets. A post
with 400 comments and no DMs grew the wrong audience.

### The worked example, flagged as a hypothesis

If the pattern holds from the drafts in `content/posts.json`, the winner will
be the loneliness post, hook: "You can love him and still feel lonely."

Why it should win, if it does: it's a permission post. It gives her language for
something contradictory she's been carrying alone, and it doesn't ask her to do
anything or admit anything publicly. Contradiction in the hook, recognition in
the body, no diagnosis anywhere. Reactions high, shares moderate because
sharing it says something about her own marriage, comments low for the same
reason, saves high.

If that's the pattern, these are the ten to try next, strongest first:

1. There's nothing wrong. That's the problem. (same contradiction structure)
2. You're not broken. You're exhausted. (permission, no action asked)
3. You don't hate sex. You hate pressure. (same shape, different subject)
4. The absence of conflict isn't presence. (contradiction, from the room)
5. Your body didn't betray you. She adapted. (permission, somatic)
6. Fighting means someone still wants something. (reframe of a relief)
7. You didn't drift apart. You got organised. (their phrase, corrected)
8. Shutting down wasn't the problem. It was the solution. (reframe)
