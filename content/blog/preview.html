Why You Can Be Best Friends And Still Not Want Each Other

You don't fight. You're kind to each other. The school runs are sorted, the mortgage is sorted, the Sunday admin is sorted. You'd call him your best friend and you'd mean it.

And you haven't wanted him in about four years.

I hear this every week. Women come into my office almost apologising for being there, because nothing is wrong. That's the part that frightens them. No affair. No shouting. No villain. Just a low, polite flatness where the heat used to be.

David Deida named that flatness back in 1995, and he wasn't especially kind about it.

He called it the second stage, and he said most of us get stuck there

In Intimate Communion (1995), Deida lays out three stages of relationship. It's still the clearest map I've found for the exact flatness I described above.

First stage. Roles by duty. He handles the money, she handles the feelings. Predictable, secure, often lonely, frequently unfair. Your grandparents' marriage, and for some of you, your parents'.

Second stage. We fixed all that. Fifty fifty. Equality, independence, negotiation. Both of you do everything. You're teammates. You're fair to each other. You genuinely like each other.

And you're flat.

Deida's point is that fairness and wanting get measured on completely different instruments. Becoming excellent colleagues doesn't produce desire. Sometimes it quietly cancels it.

Third stage. You're not together out of need, and you're not managing each other out of fairness. Both of you are free, both of you have chosen this, and you deliberately bring difference back into the room because you want the charge, not because a role says you must.

"Nothing is wrong" is second stage's signature. It's also why so many women decide they must be broken, when the problem is closer to architecture.

Polarity, which is his word for the thing nobody says out loud

Two magnets facing the same way push each other apart. Attraction needs a difference of charge.

That's the whole idea, and it's the one people get wrong fastest. Polarity is not about roles. It isn't him earning while you cook. It isn't you becoming smaller, softer, quieter, less capable so he can feel like a man. Anyone selling you that is selling you first stage in nicer packaging.

It's about energy, not chores.

I had a couple who ran their marriage like a well chaired meeting. Shared calendar, colour coded. A weekly check in with an actual agenda. Two genuinely lovely people who hadn't surprised each other in six years. Nothing in that marriage was unfair. Nothing in it was alive either.

Masculine and feminine are not men and women

Deida says this himself, over and over, and almost nobody quotes it.

He's talking about essence, not gender. Some people sit mostly in a masculine essence, some mostly feminine, some close to the middle, and it has nothing to do with what's between your legs or what you do for a living. Plenty of women have a masculine essence. That isn't a failure to be fixed.

Here's what I see in women over 40, though. Twenty years in your masculine, because that's what got the promotion, held the household, raised the teenagers, managed the aging parents. Directive, organised, on it.

The competence isn't the problem. Never being able to put it down is.

You can't run the whole operation all day and then be ravished at 9pm. Nobody can. It isn't a character flaw, it's a gear change nobody taught you how to make.

What he says to women

Dear Lover (2005) is written straight to a woman, and it's the one I'd hand you first. It's less famous than the men's book. It's better for us.

A few things from it that land in my room:

Your longing isn't a flaw. That ache you've been treating as evidence something's wrong with you? He reads it as proof your heart is still open. Women who feel nothing at all are in much more trouble.

Love is a gift you give, not a refund you're owed. Now, this one needs handling. He is not saying keep pouring yourself into a man who's harming you. He is saying don't wait to be chosen before you come back to life. Don't put your own heart on hold pending his performance.

You can feel his presence, and words won't fake it. You already know the difference between a man who's physically in the kitchen and a man who's actually there with you. Deida doesn't treat that as you being needy. He treats it as accurate instrumentation.

Your radiance isn't a dress size. He writes about a woman's light as something energetic. Not a body to be corrected.

What he says to men

The Way of the Superior Man (1997) is the famous one. Know your purpose. Stop waiting to feel ready. Don't make her the reason you never lived your life. Give her your full presence instead of your analysis. Praise her out loud.

There's also a chapter called "Her Complaint Is Content Free", which gets misused constantly.

What he means: underneath the words about the dishwasher is usually "I can't feel you". Go for that.

How it gets used: "my wife's complaints don't mean anything, Deida says so."

If your husband reads one thing, make it this book. And if he comes back quoting that chapter at you while the dishwasher stays full, he's read it wrong.

Sex as practice, not performance

In Finding God Through Sex (2002) and Blue Truth (2006), Deida goes somewhere most relationship books won't. Sex as a spiritual practice. Not technique, not performance, not a score line.

This is where he and I agree most. Orgasm isn't the goal. Presence is the goal. Pleasure and connection are the goal. He'd add devotion to that list, and I think he's onto something.

Where I'd be careful

I use his map. I don't hand anyone the book without a conversation first.

The language is dated. Written in the 1990s, heterosexual, heavily gendered, binary. His own caveat about essence not being gender is the first thing most readers lose.

That "content free" idea is dangerous in the wrong hands. In a man avoiding accountability, it becomes a licence to stop listening, wrapped in spiritual language.

Polarity can't fix an unsafe relationship. Where there's contempt, betrayal, coercion or control, polarity is absolutely the wrong tool. You don't need more charge, you need safety first. That's what the attachment work is for, and it comes before any of this.

Ravishment needs consent and a track record. He writes beautifully about being met with presence and force rather than a negotiation. Gorgeous with a man you trust completely. With a man you've been bracing against for ten years, it's a disaster. The trust has to be built before the heat.

He mostly skips the nervous system. There's almost nothing in Deida about responsive desire, or about brakes and accelerators. Polarity is an accelerator. An accelerator does nothing at all while your foot is flat on the brake, and for most women over 40 the brakes are the whole story. Exhaustion, resentment, body shame, the teenager still awake upstairs.

So: take the map. Leave the 1990s.

Three small things this week

  1. Put your competence down for twenty minutes. Not productively. Dance badly in the kitchen. Lie on the floor. Anything with no outcome attached.
  2. Stop negotiating one thing. Instead of "shall we do a date night, what works for you", try "I want you to plan something and not tell me what it is". Watch what that does to the charge in the room.
  3. Ask your body, how are you doing, darling? Then actually wait for the answer. She's been trying to tell you something for a while.

One question before you go

If nothing in your relationship is wrong, and you still feel lonely in it, what have you been telling yourself that means?

Because in my experience it doesn't mean you chose wrong, and it doesn't mean you're broken. It usually means the two of you got very good at being fair and nobody showed you what comes after that.

Why You Never Want Sex Until You're Already Having It

Let me describe your Tuesday night.

He reaches over. You do the mental maths. You're tired, your to do list is still running, and you notice something close to dread. Not of him. Of the whole production.

You say yes anyway, or you say no and feel guilty, and either way you file away one more piece of evidence that something in you has gone quiet.

And then here's the bit nobody tells you. On the nights you do say yes, twenty minutes in, you're fine. More than fine. You're enjoying it and you're wondering why you don't do this more often.

You've probably been reading that as proof you're faking it, or forcing it, or have become the kind of woman who has to talk herself into her own marriage.

It isn't. It's a well documented pattern, and a Canadian doctor named Rosemary Basson put it on paper in 2000.

First, the model you've been failing at

For decades, the official sequence of sex went like this: you want it, then you get turned on, then you come.

That comes from Masters and Johnson, Human Sexual Response (1966), who mapped excitement, plateau, orgasm and resolution. Then Helen Singer Kaplan, in Disorders of Sexual Desire (1979), stuck desire on the front as step one.

Desire first. Then arousal. Then orgasm. A straight line.

Every magazine quiz you've ever taken, every pharmaceutical advert, every bloke in a pub with an opinion, all of it runs on that line. And it has one enormous problem.

For an awful lot of women, desire does not come first. So when it doesn't show up on cue, you don't conclude that the line is wrong. You conclude that you are.

I see the damage from that every single week. Women who've spent fifteen years quietly diagnosing themselves against a model built in a laboratory on people selected because they could reliably become aroused to order.

What Basson actually said

In "The Female Sexual Response: A Different Model" (Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy, 2000), Basson proposed something different. Not a line. A circle.

Here's the circle in plain words.

You start from neutral. Not turned on, not turned off. Just getting on with your day. This is where most women in long term relationships begin, and it's completely ordinary.

Something makes you willing. And notice: the reasons are often not sexual at all. Wanting to feel close. Missing him. Wanting to be wanted. Wanting the tenderness afterwards. Basson treats those as legitimate starting points, not as second best.

Then you need a sexual stimulus, in a context that works. Touch that's actually good. Privacy. Enough time. Your body not braced. A mind that isn't running the grocery list.

Arousal arrives. Your body starts responding. And this is the whole point of her model:

Desire shows up now. After arousal, not before it.

That's responsive desire. Twenty minutes in, suddenly you want it. Nothing about that is fake and nothing about it is forced.

Then satisfaction. Physical, emotional, or both. It doesn't have to include orgasm to count, and Basson's model explicitly allows for that.

And the satisfaction feeds back into the closeness, which makes you more willing next time. Round the circle again.

So you were never missing step one. You were running a different sequence and grading yourself on somebody else's.

Willingness is not duty sex, and you need to know the difference

Now, I have to be careful here, because this model gets twisted fast.

"Desire comes after arousal" in the wrong mouth becomes "so just do it anyway and you'll warm up, love."

No.

Here's the line I hold in my room. Willingness means you're curious, you're open, you're saying yes to finding out what happens. There's an openness in your body, even a faint one. You can stop whenever you like and you know that in your bones.

Duty sex means you're absent. You're gone somewhere above the ceiling, waiting for it to be over, counting. Your no isn't available, or it costs too much to use.

One of those builds desire over months. The other one kills it, and it is the single most common cause of low desire I see in women over 40. Years of overriding yourself. Your body learns that sex means being ignored, including by you, and she starts saying no earlier and earlier.

Responsive desire is an invitation to experiment. It is not an instruction to override yourself. Ever.

Why context does most of the work

If desire comes after arousal, and arousal depends on context, then context stops being a nice extra and becomes the main event.

Which explains so much.

Why it works on holiday and not on a Tuesday. Why you need the door locked. Why resentment from Thursday's argument is still sitting in your hips on Saturday. Why you can't go from spreadsheet to sensual in four minutes. Why the teenager being awake upstairs ends the entire conversation.

This is where Basson lines up with the brakes and accelerators idea from the dual control model, which Emily Nagoski brought to a general audience in her 2015 book. Accelerators turn you on. Brakes turn you off. Most women over 40 don't have a broken accelerator. They've got a foot flat on the brake.

Fatigue. Resentment. Body shame. Pain. Pressure. Being needed all day by everyone.

You can buy all the lingerie you like. It's an accelerator, and accelerators do nothing while the brake is down.

You're not disordered

Worth knowing: when the DSM was revised in 2013, this thinking made it in. The old separate diagnoses for low desire and low arousal in women were merged into one category, and the criteria now recognise that absent spontaneous desire is not in itself a problem if desire turns up in response to arousal.

Read that again if you've been carrying a private verdict about yourself since about 2009.

The other thing to put down: your body and your mind don't always agree, and that's normal too. Meredith Chivers' work on arousal nonconcordance showed that genital response and felt desire can run on separate tracks. Wet doesn't always mean willing. Dry doesn't always mean uninterested. Neither one gets to overrule what you actually want.

Where I'd be careful

It isn't universal. Some women have plenty of spontaneous desire, and some men are strongly responsive. This isn't a women's model and a men's model, whatever the headlines did with it. If you've always wanted it out of nowhere and that's recently stopped, pay attention rather than filing it under responsive desire.

It can become a lovely excuse. "It's just responsive desire" should never be the end of the conversation when something else is going on. Pain during sex is not responsive desire. Neither is a thyroid problem, an antidepressant side effect, vaginal dryness after menopause, untreated depression, or a husband you've stopped trusting. Please get the physical things checked. A good model should open questions, not close them.

It came mostly from clinical settings, from women who'd come in with complaints, in long term relationships. It describes that situation beautifully. It was never meant to describe everyone.

What to actually do with this

  1. Stop auditing yourself for desire before you begin. That's the instrument reading you've been using, and it's the wrong gauge. Ask instead: am I willing to find out? That question is answerable on a Tuesday.
  2. Make a brakes list. Ten minutes, honestly. Everything that shuts you down. Noise, lighting, the unspoken argument, his breath, your bra, the thought that he'll want it again tomorrow. Then pick one and deal with it. One brake lifted beats a year of trying harder.
  3. Agree on a twenty minute experiment with him. You start, with no obligation to continue, and either of you can stop at any point with nothing owed. Most couples are stunned by this. It takes the pressure off, and pressure is the biggest brake of all.
  4. Warm the whole day, not the last four minutes. A text at eleven. A hand on your back while you're cooking. Responsive desire needs a runway.
  5. Ask your body, how are you doing, darling? Before you decide anything. She has information.

The papers, if you want it from the source

  • Rosemary Basson, "The Female Sexual Response: A Different Model", Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy, 2000, volume 26, pages 51 to 65. The original. Short, readable, and it changed the field: doi.org/10.1080/009262300278641
  • Rosemary Basson, "Using a Different Model for Female Sexual Response to Address Women's Problematic Low Sexual Desire", 2001. Where she turns the model into something you can actually use in a clinic.
  • Rosemary Basson, "Human Sex Response Cycles", 2001. Where she extends it to men as well.
  • "The Circles of Sex: Basson's Sex Response Cycle", in the Encyclopedia of Sexuality and Gender. A clear overview if the original is paywalled for you: link.springer.com

Books

  • Masters and Johnson, Human Sexual Response (1966), and Helen Singer Kaplan, Disorders of Sexual Desire (1979), if you want to see the model you've been held to.
  • Emily Nagoski's 2015 book on women's sexuality, for brakes and accelerators in everyday language. The dual control model underneath it came out of the Kinsey Institute.
  • Lori Brotto, Better Sex Through Mindfulness: How Women Can Cultivate Desire (2018), and the workbook that followed in 2022. She directs the sexual health laboratory at the same university as Basson: loribrotto.com

Watch

  • Emily Nagoski, "The truth about unwanted arousal", TED, 2018. Fifteen minutes on arousal nonconcordance, and the clearest explanation of it anywhere: ted.com
  • "Mind Full: Better Sex Through Mindfulness with Dr Lori Brotto": youtube.com

Blogs and articles worth your time

  • Dr Jason Winters, "The Sexual Response Cycle and Why It Matters". A sex therapist walking through all three models in plain language: drjasonwinters.com
  • "Why Does My Partner Want Sex Faster Than I Do?", The Psychology Group. Useful if the mismatch in your house is the thing that hurts most: thepsychologygroup.com
  • "What Basson's Sexual Response Cycle Teaches Us About Sexuality", KPACT. Short, and good on why the model matters beyond straight couples: kpact.xyz

One last thing

You are not a woman who lost her desire.

You're a woman who was handed the wrong instructions, tried to follow them for twenty years, and concluded she was the faulty one.

Different sequence. Same body. She's still in there, and she's been waiting for somebody to stop grading her.

Are you willing to find out?

Five things to sign off

Flagged rather than quietly decided, so none of it reaches the live blog by accident.

  • Click every outbound link once

    The books, papers and DOIs are solid. The YouTube links and the two blog articles were found by search and look right, but they haven't been watched or read end to end, and a dead link in a reading list is the thing readers notice.

  • The client story in the Deida post is invented

    The couple with the colour coded shared calendar is a composite illustration, not a client. Swap it for one of your own, or cut it.

    Emily Nagoski's 2015 book is referred to by author and year only, because its title is on the banned phrase list in the voice checker. Two places to put it back if you're happy to use it in a reading list.

  • The Basson post cites a diagnostic manual

    The 2013 DSM revision, and what it now says about absent spontaneous desire. Accurate and kept non clinical in tone, but the whole section can go without breaking the piece.

    The post says so out loud, in its own section, which is the only honest way to reference him for women over 40. Worth a read to decide whether that goes stronger or softer.
