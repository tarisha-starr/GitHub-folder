# Why You Never Want Sex Until You're Already Having It

Let me describe your Tuesday night.

He reaches over. You do the mental maths. You're tired, your to do list is still
running, and you notice something close to dread. Not of him. Of the whole
production.

You say yes anyway, or you say no and feel guilty, and either way you file away
one more piece of evidence that something in you has gone quiet.

And then here's the bit nobody tells you. On the nights you do say yes, twenty
minutes in, you're fine. More than fine. You're enjoying it and you're wondering
why you don't do this more often.

You've probably been reading that as proof you're faking it, or forcing it, or
have become the kind of woman who has to talk herself into her own marriage.

It isn't. It's a well documented pattern, and a Canadian doctor named Rosemary
Basson put it on paper in 2000.

## First, the model you've been failing at

For decades, the official sequence of sex went like this: you want it, then you
get turned on, then you come.

That comes from Masters and Johnson, *Human Sexual Response* (1966), who mapped
excitement, plateau, orgasm and resolution. Then Helen Singer Kaplan, in
*Disorders of Sexual Desire* (1979), stuck desire on the front as step one.

Desire first. Then arousal. Then orgasm. A straight line.

Every magazine quiz you've ever taken, every pharmaceutical advert, every bloke
in a pub with an opinion, all of it runs on that line. And it has one enormous
problem.

For an awful lot of women, desire does not come first. So when it doesn't show
up on cue, you don't conclude that the line is wrong. You conclude that you are.

I see the damage from that every single week. Women who've spent fifteen years
quietly diagnosing themselves against a model built in a laboratory on people
selected because they could reliably become aroused to order.

## What Basson actually said

In "The Female Sexual Response: A Different Model" (Journal of Sex & Marital
Therapy, 2000), Basson proposed something different. Not a line. A circle.

Here's the circle in plain words.

**You start from neutral.** Not turned on, not turned off. Just getting on with
your day. This is where most women in long term relationships begin, and it's
completely ordinary.

**Something makes you willing.** And notice: the reasons are often not sexual at
all. Wanting to feel close. Missing him. Wanting to be wanted. Wanting the
tenderness afterwards. Basson treats those as legitimate starting points, not as
second best.

**Then you need a sexual stimulus, in a context that works.** Touch that's
actually good. Privacy. Enough time. Your body not braced. A mind that isn't
running the grocery list.

**Arousal arrives.** Your body starts responding. And this is the whole point of
her model:

**Desire shows up now. After arousal, not before it.**

That's responsive desire. Twenty minutes in, suddenly you want it. Nothing about
that is fake and nothing about it is forced.

**Then satisfaction.** Physical, emotional, or both. It doesn't have to include
orgasm to count, and Basson's model explicitly allows for that.

**And the satisfaction feeds back into the closeness,** which makes you more
willing next time. Round the circle again.

So you were never missing step one. You were running a different sequence and
grading yourself on somebody else's.

## Willingness is not duty sex, and you need to know the difference

Now, I have to be careful here, because this model gets twisted fast.

"Desire comes after arousal" in the wrong mouth becomes "so just do it anyway and
you'll warm up, love."

No.

Here's the line I hold in my room. Willingness means you're curious, you're open,
you're saying yes to finding out what happens. There's an openness in your body,
even a faint one. You can stop whenever you like and you know that in your bones.

Duty sex means you're absent. You're gone somewhere above the ceiling, waiting
for it to be over, counting. Your no isn't available, or it costs too much to
use.

One of those builds desire over months. The other one kills it, and it is the
single most common cause of low desire I see in women over 40. Years of
overriding yourself. Your body learns that sex means being ignored, including by
you, and she starts saying no earlier and earlier.

Responsive desire is an invitation to experiment. It is not an instruction to
override yourself. Ever.

## Why context does most of the work

If desire comes after arousal, and arousal depends on context, then context stops
being a nice extra and becomes the main event.

Which explains so much.

Why it works on holiday and not on a Tuesday. Why you need the door locked. Why
resentment from Thursday's argument is still sitting in your hips on Saturday.
Why you can't go from spreadsheet to sensual in four minutes. Why the teenager
being awake upstairs ends the entire conversation.

This is where Basson lines up with the brakes and accelerators idea from the dual
control model, which Emily Nagoski brought to a general audience in her 2015
book. Accelerators turn you on. Brakes turn you off. Most women over 40 don't
have a broken accelerator. They've got a foot flat on the brake.

Fatigue. Resentment. Body shame. Pain. Pressure. Being needed all day by
everyone.

You can buy all the lingerie you like. It's an accelerator, and accelerators do
nothing while the brake is down.

## You're not disordered

Worth knowing: when the DSM was revised in 2013, this thinking made it in. The
old separate diagnoses for low desire and low arousal in women were merged into
one category, and the criteria now recognise that absent spontaneous desire is
not in itself a problem if desire turns up in response to arousal.

Read that again if you've been carrying a private verdict about yourself since
about 2009.

The other thing to put down: your body and your mind don't always agree, and
that's normal too. Meredith Chivers' work on arousal nonconcordance showed that
genital response and felt desire can run on separate tracks. Wet doesn't always
mean willing. Dry doesn't always mean uninterested. Neither one gets to overrule
what you actually want.

## Where I'd be careful

**It isn't universal.** Some women have plenty of spontaneous desire, and some men
are strongly responsive. This isn't a women's model and a men's model, whatever
the headlines did with it. If you've always wanted it out of nowhere and that's
recently stopped, pay attention rather than filing it under responsive desire.

**It can become a lovely excuse.** "It's just responsive desire" should never be
the end of the conversation when something else is going on. Pain during sex is
not responsive desire. Neither is a thyroid problem, an antidepressant side
effect, vaginal dryness after menopause, untreated depression, or a husband
you've stopped trusting. Please get the physical things checked. A good model
should open questions, not close them.

**It came mostly from clinical settings,** from women who'd come in with
complaints, in long term relationships. It describes that situation beautifully.
It was never meant to describe everyone.

## What to actually do with this

**Stop auditing yourself for desire before you begin.** That's the instrument
reading you've been using, and it's the wrong gauge. Ask instead: am I willing to
find out? That question is answerable on a Tuesday.

**Make a brakes list.** Ten minutes, honestly. Everything that shuts you down.
Noise, lighting, the unspoken argument, his breath, your bra, the thought that
he'll want it again tomorrow. Then pick one and deal with it. One brake lifted
beats a year of trying harder.

**Agree on a twenty minute experiment with him.** You start, with no obligation
to continue, and either of you can stop at any point with nothing owed. Most
couples are stunned by this. It takes the pressure off, and pressure is the
biggest brake of all.

**Warm the whole day, not the last four minutes.** A text at eleven. A hand on
your back while you're cooking. Responsive desire needs a runway.

**Ask your body, how are you doing, darling?** Before you decide anything. She
has information.

## Where to go next

### The papers, if you want it from the source

- Rosemary Basson, "The Female Sexual Response: A Different Model", Journal of
  Sex & Marital Therapy, 2000, volume 26, pages 51 to 65. The original. Short,
  readable, and it changed the field:
  [doi.org/10.1080/009262300278641](https://doi.org/10.1080/009262300278641)
- Rosemary Basson, "Using a Different Model for Female Sexual Response to Address
  Women's Problematic Low Sexual Desire", Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy, 2001.
  This is the one where she turns the model into something you can actually use
  in a clinic.
- Rosemary Basson, "Human Sex Response Cycles", Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy,
  2001. Where she extends it to men as well.
- "The Circles of Sex: Basson's Sex Response Cycle", in the Encyclopedia of
  Sexuality and Gender. A clear academic overview if the original paper is behind
  a paywall for you:
  [link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-319-59531-3_37-1)

### Books

- Masters and Johnson, *Human Sexual Response* (1966), and Helen Singer Kaplan,
  *Disorders of Sexual Desire* (1979), if you want to see the model you've been
  held to.
- Emily Nagoski's 2015 book on women's sexuality, for brakes and accelerators in
  everyday language. The dual control model underneath it came out of the Kinsey
  Institute.
- Lori Brotto, *Better Sex Through Mindfulness: How Women Can Cultivate Desire*
  (2018), and the workbook that followed in 2022. She directs the sexual health
  laboratory at the same university as Basson, and it's the practical companion
  piece: [loribrotto.com](https://www.loribrotto.com/)

## One last thing

You are not a woman who lost her desire.

You're a woman who was handed the wrong instructions, tried to follow them for
twenty years, and concluded she was the faulty one.

Different sequence. Same body. She's still in there, and she's been waiting for
somebody to stop grading her.

Are you willing to find out?
