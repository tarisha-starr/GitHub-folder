A woman sat in my office last month and said, “I think something in me is broken. I used to want it. Now I don’t.”

She’s 51. Two teenagers who need lifts everywhere. A job that eats the good hours. A husband who’s stopped asking, which somehow stings more than being asked.

And she’d decided the fault was hers. One thing. One broken part. Like a dishwasher.

It’s almost never one thing, my love.

Low libido in women over 40 gets talked about as though it were a single broken part. In the room, it never is. It’s usually five things, and four of them have nothing to do with your body.

Someone finally wrote the whole list down

There’s a paper I keep coming back to. It came out in the Journal of Marital and Family Therapy in 2023, and the model in it was developed by Abby Girard, Nicholas Newstrom, Jennifer Connor, Katherine Arenella, Jennifer Vencill and Beatrice “Bean” Robinson. Couple and family therapists and psychologists, most of them out of the Institute for Sexual and Gender Health in Minnesota, all of them people who treat sexual problems for a living.

They call it the SHIP model. Sexual Health and Integrative Pleasure.

Yes. The name sounds like a filing cabinet. Stay with me anyway.

What Girard and her colleagues did is genuinely useful. They took everything a good sex therapist is quietly holding in her head during a session, and they laid it out flat. Five lenses around the outside. Five things the work actually touches in the middle.

Ten pieces. And when I read that list, I recognised every single woman who’s ever cried on my couch.

First, the five lenses underneath low desire

These are the five foundations Girard and her colleagues put underneath the whole model. You don’t need to memorise them. You just need to notice that not one of them is “what’s wrong with her.”

You’re in a system. You’re not a problem floating in space. You’re in a relationship, a household, a family, a whole ecosystem of who does the washing and who apologises first.

Your life shaped what you were allowed to want. Your culture. Your faith, or the one you were raised in. Your race, your body size, your age, who you love. All of it wrote rules into you before you could read. They call this intersectionality.

Body, mind, world and culture, all at once. Hormones and resentment and the mortgage and what your mother’s church said about pleasure. Not one of them. All of it. At the same time.

Your sexuality has a lifespan. You at 51 aren’t a broken version of you at 25. You’re a different woman with different wiring and different needs, and she’s never been listened to properly.

And check what’s actually true. Not what everybody assumes. Not what your mother implied. What’s true, for you, right now. They call that empiricism, which is a big word for “look, don’t guess.”

Sit with that for a second. Five lenses, and “she’s faulty” isn’t one of them.

Then, the five things sex therapy actually works on

These five sit in the middle of the circle, and they’re the heart of what Girard, Newstrom, Connor, Arenella, Vencill and Robinson built. This is the part I want you to keep.

1. Sexual literacy

You were never taught. That’s it. That’s the whole thing.

I sit with women who can list every allergen in a school lunchbox and can’t name the parts of their own vulva. Who genuinely don’t know that most women don’t come from penetration. Who thought wanting sex is supposed to arrive first, like hunger, and when it doesn’t they assume they’re finished. It isn’t a fault: female sexual response is individual, and yours is normal.

You’re not thick. You were handed a diagram of a fallopian tube at 13 and nothing since.

Learning the actual information changes women faster than almost anything else I do. If you want somewhere to start, this walks you through it: female pleasure anatomy for beginners.

2. Sexual adaptation and resilience

Bodies change. Babies change them. Surgery, menopause, illness, antidepressants, grief, a bad year, a terrible year.

The question that keeps women stuck is “how do I get back to how I was.”

The question that frees them is “what works for this body, now?” If menopause is part of your picture, start here: hormones, menopause and sexuality.

Adaptation isn’t settling. It’s the skill. The couples who last aren’t the ones nothing happened to. They’re the ones who kept renegotiating.

3. Relational intimacy

Desire doesn’t live in the bedroom, darling. It lives in the fourteen hours before it.

It lives in whether he noticed you were flat at breakfast. Whether you felt like a person or a service today. Whether the thing you said three weeks ago ever got heard.

“We don’t even fight, so why am I so lonely?”

Because the absence of conflict isn’t presence. Two polite strangers sharing a mortgage isn’t intimacy.

If you and your partner want it at different rates, that has its own map: sexual desire discrepancy help for couples.

4. Pleasure as the point

Not performance. Not a result. Not finishing.

Orgasm isn’t the goal. I’ll say it as many times as it takes. The moment pleasure becomes a target you have to hit, your body reads it as a test, and no body has ever relaxed into a test.

What Girard and her colleagues are naming here matters: sexuality that’s oriented towards pleasure, satisfaction and consent, rather than away from dysfunction. There’s a difference between “stop being broken” and “come home to what feels good.” Only one of those is possible.

5. Bring in the other people

Sometimes it IS the hormones. Sometimes it’s the pelvic floor. Sometimes it’s the medication that saved your life and flattened your desire, and nobody warned you.

Girard and her colleagues are firm about this one. Good care is a team. GP, pelvic floor physio, sometimes an endocrinologist, sometimes a therapist like me. Physical, psychological, relational, social, cultural and spiritual, held together rather than passed around.

You deserve to be properly examined, not just talked at. And you’re allowed to ask for more than “it’s normal at your age.”

Why any of this matters to you on a Tuesday night

Because it turns one locked door into five.

When you believe the problem is your libido, there’s one thing to fix and you can’t fix it. So you lie there feeling like a failure, and the feeling makes it worse, and round it goes.

  • If it’s information you were never given, that’s learnable.
  • If it’s a body that’s changed, that’s adaptable.
  • If it’s the loneliness in your marriage, that’s workable.
  • If it’s pressure to perform, that’s droppable.
  • If it’s your thyroid, that’s treatable.

Five doors. If one’s stuck, try another one.

Nothing about you is broken. There are just five conversations nobody ever had with you, and you’ve been carrying the shame for all five.

Put it down.

Questions women ask me about low libido after 40

Is low libido after 40 normal?

Common, yes. Normal in the sense of “nothing to be done about it”, no. Desire shifts with hormones, sleep, medication, resentment and how safe you feel. Common and permanent are different words. More here: is low desire normal?

Can low libido be fixed?

“Fixed” assumes a fault. What actually happens is that one of the five doors opens. Most women I work with find it’s the one they were least expecting, usually not the physical one.

Is it my hormones or is it my relationship?

Often both, which is exactly why the model holds body and relationship together rather than making you choose. Get the bloods done and look at the fourteen hours before bed. One doesn’t rule out the other.

How long does it take to want sex again?

It depends which door. Information changes things in weeks. A relationship pattern takes longer, and it moves. What doesn’t work is waiting for desire to come back on its own while nothing else changes. How to want sex after 40 walks through the first steps.
