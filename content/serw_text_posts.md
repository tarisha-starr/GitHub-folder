# SERW Text Posts

Twenty text only posts for the SERW audience: women over 40 around desire,
intimacy, body and midlife. No image needed. The post earns the click, the
comments do the teaching.

## The formula

1. Open with a clear problem, result or surprising fact.
2. Say why it matters in one short sentence.
3. Promise the useful breakdown in the comments.
4. Use the comments to teach the complete process. At least seven comments,
   posted as separate comments, in order.
5. Reply to genuine questions and add useful examples.

## How to post these

- Post the text post, then drop comment 1 within sixty seconds. Don't leave a
  promise sitting there empty.
- One step per comment. Seven to nine comments. Separate comments, not one
  block. Each one is another reason for the post to resurface.
- Number them (1/8, 2/8) so people know there's more coming and scroll for it.
- Answer every genuine question with a specific example, not "great question".
- Voice rules from `content/style_guide.md` apply: contractions, NZ spelling,
  no dashes, short lines, no coaching cliches.
- Two reply examples are written under each post. Use them as the tone, not as
  a script.

## 1. Low desire isn't broken

**Post**

> Most women think low desire means something's broken in them.
>
> For most of my clients it's the opposite. Desire is working exactly as
> designed, it just doesn't switch on the way we were taught it would.
>
> Here's how it actually works 👇🏽👇🏽

**Comments**

1/9 There are two kinds of desire. Spontaneous: you want it out of nowhere.
Responsive: you want it once something good is already happening. Both are
normal. Neither is the broken one.

2/9 Most women over 40 run responsive. So waiting to "feel like it" first means
waiting for a switch you don't have. You're not failing the test, you're
sitting the wrong exam.

3/9 Step one. Stop testing yourself at 10pm when you're wrecked. That's not
data, that's a tired body saying no to one more job.

4/9 Step two. Track for seven days. Write down every moment your body felt even
five percent open. Hot shower. Sun on your skin. A good laugh. Dancing while
you cook.

5/9 Step three. Look at the list. Two or three conditions will repeat. Those
are your on ramps. Everyone's are different, and yours are yours.

6/9 Step four. Book twenty minutes of touch with no destination. Nothing has to
lead anywhere. You're testing whether interest arrives after contact, not
before it.

7/9 Step five. Say the rule out loud first: "I want to start without deciding
where it ends." That one sentence takes the audition out of the room.

8/9 Step six. Do it three times before you judge it. Once is a mood. Three
times is a pattern, and a pattern is something you can work with.

9/9 If nothing arrives after three honest goes, that's information too. Usually
it's pain, exhaustion or resentment underneath, and each of those has its own
path. Tell me which one sounds like you and I'll point you at the next step.

**Replies**

> "What if he hears no destination and then expects it to go somewhere anyway?"
>
> Say it before you start, not halfway through. Before: it's an agreement.
> Halfway: it sounds like a rejection. And if it does go somewhere, that was
> your yes, not the plan.

> "Am I stuck being responsive forever?"
>
> No, but responsive isn't a lesser version. Once your on ramps are known and
> used, most women find the wanting shows up earlier and earlier. It just
> stopped arriving unannounced.

## 2. "Not tonight" isn't about him

**Post**

> When she says "not tonight", most men hear "not you".
>
> Nine times out of ten she isn't turning down her partner, she's turning down
> the script that starts the second she says yes.
>
> Here's how to change the script 👇🏽👇🏽

**Comments**

1/8 First, name what the yes currently costs. For a lot of women, yes means the
next forty minutes are already decided, and there's no exit that doesn't hurt
someone's feelings.

2/8 Step one. Write down what you're actually declining. Not "sex". Be exact.
The late hour. The same order every time. The feeling of being finished off
like a task.

3/8 Step two. Pick one of those, only one, to change first. Trying to fix all
of it in one conversation turns into a fight about the whole marriage.

4/8 Step three. Say it as a want, not a complaint. "I want it earlier" lands.
"You always start when I'm half asleep" starts a defence.

5/8 Step four. Give him a yes he can actually reach. "Not tonight, but Saturday
morning, and I mean it." A no with a door in it isn't a rejection.

6/8 Step five. Agree that either of you can stop at any point with no
explanation owed. Sounds like it would kill it. It's the opposite. Nobody
relaxes in a room they can't leave.

7/8 Step six. Debrief the next day, out of bed, fully dressed, five minutes.
"What did you like, what would you change." Bedroom debriefs turn into
performance reviews.

8/8 Step seven. Repeat for four weeks before you decide anything about your
relationship. You're rewriting a habit that's been running for years.

**Replies**

> "We tried talking about it and it turned into a three hour fight."
>
> Then it was too big and too late at night. Twenty minutes, daytime, one topic
> only, and a timer. When the timer goes, you stop even if it was going well.

> "What if I genuinely don't want it any earlier, or at all?"
>
> Then that's the honest starting point and it deserves a proper look, not a
> workaround. Pain, exhaustion and resentment all read as "not at all". Which
> one's loudest for you?

## 3. Painful sex is common, and it is treatable

**Post**

> Sex that hurts after 40 is common. It is not something you have to accept.
>
> Most women wait years before they mention it, and by then their body has
> learned to brace before anything's even started.
>
> Here's the order I work through it 👇🏽👇🏽

**Comments**

1/9 Please start here: pain during sex is a medical question first, a
relationship question second. Book the GP or a pelvic health physio. Nothing
below replaces that.

2/9 Step one. Find out where. Entrance, or deep. Those are two different
stories with two different fixes, and "it just hurts" won't get you the right
help.

3/9 Step two. Find out when. Every time, or since a specific thing. Birth,
surgery, a hard season, perimenopause. Timing points at the cause.

4/9 Step three. Ask about the tissue. Hormonal changes thin and dry things out,
and there's effective treatment for that. Ask specifically about vaginal
oestrogen and whether it's right for you.

5/9 Step four. Use proper lubricant, generously, every time, and stop treating
it as a confession. It isn't a report card on your desire.

6/9 Step five. Deal with the bracing. After months of pain your pelvic floor
grips before anything happens. That grip makes the pain worse, which teaches
more gripping. It's a loop, and it needs its own work.

7/9 Step six. Take penetration off the table completely while you're healing.
Not as a punishment. Your body will not stop bracing while it's still waiting
for the part that hurt.

8/9 Step seven. Rebuild in stages. Touch that feels good, and stop while it
still feels good. You're teaching her that this is safe again, and she only
learns that by evidence.

9/9 Step eight. Bring your partner in as an ally, with the plan written down.
Guessing partners get careful and distant, and careful and distant feels like
rejection to both of you.

**Replies**

> "My doctor told me to have a glass of wine and relax."
>
> Get a different doctor. Ask for one with menopause or pelvic pain experience,
> or ask a pelvic health physio directly who they refer to.

> "How long does the rebuilding usually take?"
>
> Weeks to months, and it moves faster when penetration is genuinely off the
> table rather than officially off the table. Bodies can tell the difference.

## 4. The mental load is a libido problem

**Post**

> You can't run the whole house in your head and then switch into wanting.
>
> The mental load isn't just tiring. It keeps you in the exact state that makes
> arousal impossible.
>
> Here's how to unhook it 👇🏽👇🏽

**Comments**

1/8 Arousal needs your attention off duty. If part of you is tracking the
permission slip, the dog food and your mother's appointment, there's nothing
left over to feel with.

2/8 Step one. Get it out of your head and onto paper. All of it. The list is
usually longer than either of you thought, and that's the point.

3/8 Step two. Mark each item with who notices it needs doing. Not who does it.
Who remembers. That column is the mental load, and it's usually one name.

4/8 Step three. Hand over three items completely. Noticing, planning, doing,
all of it. Not "remind me and I'll do it". That's still yours.

5/8 Step four. Let them be done differently, and worse, for a month. If you
take them back the first time it's wrong, you've just taught everyone that you
can't be handed anything.

6/8 Step five. Build a shutdown ritual. Ten minutes where you write tomorrow's
list and close the laptop. Your body needs a signal that the shift has ended.

7/8 Step six. Put twenty minutes of nothing between the shutdown and any
touching. Not scrolling. Nothing. A bath, the garden, lying on the floor.

8/8 Step seven. Watch what changes over three weeks. Most women notice interest
returning on the days they were genuinely off duty by 8pm, and that's the real
finding.

**Replies**

> "He says just tell me what to do and I'll do it."
>
> That sentence is the problem in one line. Being the one who tells is the job.
> Give away whole areas, not tasks.

> "I don't have twenty minutes of nothing anywhere in my week."
>
> Then that's the honest answer to why desire's gone quiet, and it's a
> scheduling problem before it's a bedroom one. Find one evening. Start there.

## 5. Faking it teaches the wrong lesson

**Post**

> Every time you fake it, you're training your partner to keep doing the thing
> that isn't working.
>
> Most women start faking to be kind. Years later they're stuck with a lover
> who's confident about all the wrong moves.
>
> Here's how to walk it back without wrecking anyone 👇🏽👇🏽

**Comments**

1/9 First, drop the guilt. You did it to protect the moment, or him, or your
own privacy. That's a reason, not a character flaw. What matters is stopping.

2/9 Step one. Stop adding new ones. You don't have to confess anything today,
just stop building on it tonight.

3/9 Step two. Learn what does work, on your own, with no audience. You can't
guide someone toward something you've never mapped.

4/9 Step three. Get specific in your own head first. Pressure or lightness.
Steady or changing. Where exactly. "Slower" and "softer" are the two most
useful words most couples never use.

5/9 Step four. Redirect in the moment instead of announcing history. "I love
this, do it slower." Present tense. No confession required.

6/9 Step five. Praise loudly when it's right. Sounds obvious. Most people
correct in silence and enjoy in silence, and their partner learns nothing.

7/9 Step six. If you want to say it out loud, say it out of bed and make it
about the future. "I've been going along with things that don't do much for me.
I want to show you what does."

8/9 Step seven. Expect him to feel it. Let him have his moment without managing
it for him. It passes, and what's on the other side is better for both of you.

9/9 Step eight. Take penetration off as the finish line for a month. Faking
usually lives right there, at the part that's supposed to end things.

**Replies**

> "I've faked it for fifteen years. Isn't it too cruel to say now?"
>
> Kinder than fifteen more. And you don't have to hand him a history. "Here's
> what I want now" is a complete sentence.

> "What if I don't actually know what I like?"
>
> Then that's step one and it's a good place to start, not an embarrassing one.
> Curiosity, no goal, no clock, and pay attention to what you'd like more of.

## 6. The lights off problem

**Post**

> If you can only do it in the dark, it isn't shyness. It's shame doing its job.
>
> And shame doesn't only hide your body. It cuts you off from feeling anything
> in it.
>
> Here's the way back 👇🏽👇🏽

**Comments**

1/8 Notice what actually happens when the light goes on. You leave. You start
watching yourself from the outside, checking the angle, the stomach, the arms.
That's the thing to work on, not the lighting.

2/8 Step one. Name the specific thought. Not "I hate my body". The actual
sentence. "He'll notice my stomach has changed." Vague shame can't be argued
with. A sentence can.

3/8 Step two. Get back inside your body before you go anywhere near a mirror.
Feet on the floor, slow breath out, feel your own hands on your own arms.
Sensation first, looking second.

4/8 Step three. Practise looking with touch, not judgement. Hands on skin, eyes
following your hands. You're feeling, not inspecting.

5/8 Step four. Use the dimmer, not the switch. Candle, lamp in the corner, dawn
light. Full dark to full light is too big a jump and you'll just prove yourself
right.

6/8 Step five. Change the input. Follow bodies that look like yours until your
eyes stop treating your own as the exception. Your standard of normal is built
from what you look at.

7/8 Step six. Let yourself be told. When he says something and you swat it
away, you're teaching him to stop saying it. Try "thank you" and nothing else,
even while you don't believe it.

8/8 Step seven. Track sensation instead of appearance for a whole month. What
felt good, where, how long. Notice how much less you thought about the light.

**Replies**

> "I've felt this way since I was fourteen. Can it really change at 52?"
>
> Yes, and often faster at 52, because you're no longer trying to be looked at.
> You're trying to feel something. Different goal, easier work.

> "What if his comments are the problem, not my head?"
>
> Then that's a different conversation and it needs to happen out of the
> bedroom. Say the sentence he said, say what it did, and ask for it to stop.

## 7. Initiating without auditioning

**Post**

> Most women stopped initiating because being turned down feels like a verdict
> on your whole self.
>
> So they wait, and then feel unwanted for waiting.
>
> Here's how to ask so a no doesn't cost you anything 👇🏽👇🏽

**Comments**

1/8 The problem isn't asking. It's that the current ask has only two answers,
yes or a rejection of you. Fix the ask and the fear goes down on its own.

2/8 Step one. Agree on a signal in advance, out of bed. A word, an emoji, a
candle you light. Nobody has to build the courage from scratch at 9pm.

3/8 Step two. Agree what a no means before you ever use it. "No means not now,
and I'll come back to you." Then keep that promise, both of you.

4/8 Step three. Ask for the on ramp, not the finish. "Come lie with me for
twenty minutes." Easier to say, easier to say yes to, and it's where responsive
desire starts anyway.

5/8 Step four. Ask earlier. 9pm is negotiating with two exhausted people. Sunday
at 4pm gets a completely different answer from the same two people.

6/8 Step five. Ask twice a week for a month, whatever the answers are. You're
building tolerance for the ask, not chasing a hit rate.

7/8 Step six. When you get a no, do nothing with it that night. No sulking, no
withdrawing, no bringing it up. That's the whole practice.

8/8 Step seven. Review it after four weeks. Most couples find the yes rate went
up, but the bigger change is that asking stopped feeling like a risk.

**Replies**

> "I've asked for months and got a no every time. When do I stop?"
>
> When you're asking into a void, stop asking and start talking. Not about that
> night, about the pattern. Both of you, daytime, with the question "what's
> actually going on for you here."

> "What if I'm too proud to ask after all these years?"
>
> Then start with the signal. It's an ask you don't have to say out loud, and it
> gets your pride out of the way while the habit rebuilds.

## 8. Arousal takes longer now, and that's normal

**Post**

> Your body takes longer to warm up at 48 than it did at 28.
>
> That isn't decline. Nobody told us the timing changes, so women assume the
> feeling has gone when it just hasn't arrived yet.
>
> Here's how to work with the new timing 👇🏽👇🏽

**Comments**

1/8 Blood flow, hormones and tissue all change through perimenopause. Everything
still works. It just needs more time and better conditions than it used to.

2/8 Step one. Stop judging your body in the first five minutes. That's the exact
window where nothing's happened yet, and it's where most women decide they're
broken.

3/8 Step two. Add twenty minutes to whatever you thought was enough. Most
couples are stopping right before the point where it would've started working.

4/8 Step three. Use lubricant from the start, not as a rescue halfway through.
Dryness stops the build, and the build is the whole thing you're waiting for.

5/8 Step four. Add heat. Warm shower, warm room, warm hands. Cold feet genuinely
stall arousal, and it's the least romantic fix that actually works.

6/8 Step five. Get moving during the day. Walking, dancing, lifting, anything
that gets blood moving. Bodies that moved that day warm up faster that night.

7/8 Step six. Change the measure. Stop asking "did I want it". Ask "did my body
get more awake than it was". That's the honest signal now.

8/8 Step seven. Track it for a month. Time to warm up, what helped, what didn't.
You'll get your own number, and knowing it takes the panic out.

**Replies**

> "Twenty extra minutes, in whose life?"
>
> Fair. Which is why this belongs on Saturday morning, not Tuesday at 10pm. The
> timing isn't the problem, the slot is.

> "I get warmed up and then it just stops."
>
> Very common, and usually a break getting pressed. A noise, a thought, a
> deadline in your head. Next time notice what happened in the ten seconds
> before it went, and tell me what you find.

## 9. Months without touch

**Post**

> Some couples come to me having not touched in two years. Neither of them ever
> decided it.
>
> Nobody stops on purpose. It's one tired month, then a run of them, then the
> silence gets its own weight.
>
> Here's how it restarts 👇🏽👇🏽

**Comments**

1/9 First, know that this is much more common than either of you thinks. You're
not the only house in the street where this is going on.

2/9 Step one. Say it out loud once, without blame. "We haven't touched in a long
time and I miss it." No history, no scoreboard, one sentence.

3/9 Step two. Take sex off the table for three weeks, out loud and agreed. The
pressure is what's kept you both away from the couch.

4/9 Step three. Start with non sexual touch every day. Twenty seconds of a real
hug. Hand on the back walking past. Feet in a lap on the sofa.

5/9 Step four. Add one longer thing a week. Fifteen minutes of shoulders, or
lying together with no phones. Put it in the calendar or it won't happen.

6/9 Step five. Expect it to feel awkward for the first fortnight. Awkward is
what re entry feels like. Most couples quit right there and call it proof.

7/9 Step six. Keep talking outside the bedroom. Five minutes, twice a week,
about how it's going. The silence is what let it drift the first time.

8/9 Step seven. Only after touch feels normal again, open the question of
what's next. Ask what each of you wants now, not what you used to do.

9/9 Step eight. If it stalls in the same place every time, there's usually
something under it. Pain, resentment, grief, a hurt nobody named. That's the
work, and it's worth doing rather than waiting out.

**Replies**

> "Two years for us. Is that too far gone?"
>
> No. I've watched couples come back from longer. What predicts it isn't the
> length, it's whether both of you are willing to be awkward for a fortnight.

> "What if I ask and he says he's fine as we are?"
>
> Then ask the second question, gently: "Fine, or given up?" Most people who say
> fine have quietly filed it under things that can't be changed.

## 10. Talking about sex without a fight

**Post**

> Most couples only talk about sex right after it went badly.
>
> That's the worst possible moment, and it's why the conversation always turns
> into a fight.
>
> Here's the structure that works 👇🏽👇🏽

**Comments**

1/8 Rule one: never in bed, never straight after, never during a fight about
something else. Kitchen table, daytime, both dressed.

2/8 Step one. Book it like anything else. "Sunday, 4pm, twenty minutes." A
scheduled talk stops it from ambushing whoever's least ready.

3/8 Step two. Set a timer for twenty minutes and stop when it goes, even mid
flow. Long talks turn into inventories, and inventories turn into blame.

4/8 Step three. One topic. Frequency, or initiating, or what happens in the
first ten minutes. Not all three, and not "us".

5/8 Step four. Each of you gets five minutes uninterrupted. The other one's job
is to listen, then say back what they heard. Not to answer it yet.

6/8 Step five. Speak in wants, not faults. "I want more time before" instead of
"you rush me". Same information, completely different room.

7/8 Step six. Finish with one small experiment for the week. One. Specific.
Something you can actually tell whether you did or didn't.

8/8 Step seven. Same time next week, and start by reviewing the experiment.
Four of these in a row will do more than one enormous conversation ever has.

**Replies**

> "He shuts down two minutes in every time."
>
> Then two minutes is his current limit, so make the talk five minutes and stay
> inside it. Shutting down usually means flooded, not uninterested.

> "Can we do this over text? It's easier."
>
> For the booking, yes. For the talk, no. Text has no tone and infinite time to
> reread, and both of those make it worse.

## 11. The one who wants it more isn't the problem

> In most couples, one wants it more. That gap isn't a fault in either of you.
>
> What wrecks couples isn't the gap. It's what they both make it mean.
>
> Here's how to hold it without either of you becoming the villain 👇🏽👇🏽

**Comments**

1/8 Right now one of you is "the one who always wants it" and the other is "the
one who always says no". Both of those are miserable jobs and neither is true.

2/8 Step one. Both of you say out loud what the gap has come to mean. Usually
"I'm too much" on one side and "I'm never enough" on the other. Say it, hear it.

3/8 Step two. Separate touch from sex, out loud. Most higher desire partners
have stopped touching at all in case it reads as asking. That's why the house
feels cold.

4/8 Step three. Agree a floor and a ceiling. A rhythm you're both fine with, and
a limit that's honoured without a fight. Written down, not implied.

5/8 Step four. Let the lower desire partner do the initiating for a month.
Nothing changes the dynamic faster, and it gets the wanting out of one person's
hands.

6/8 Step five. Agree what a no sounds like, and what comes with it. "Not
tonight, and I do want you" is a completely different sentence from "not
tonight" said to the ceiling.

7/8 Step six. Take orgasm off as the definition. Some nights are twenty minutes
of skin. If the only thing that counts is the full event, you'll keep scoring
yourselves as failing.

8/8 Step seven. Review monthly, not nightly. Nightly scorekeeping is what turned
a difference into a grievance in the first place.

**Replies**

> "I'm the one who wants it more and I feel like a nuisance."

partner could have guessed.

## 13. She braces before you've even started

**Post**

> Your pelvic floor grips before anything's happened. You don't decide it, and
> you often don't feel it.
>
> A gripped body can't open, so it hurts, so it grips harder. That's a loop, and
> it needs its own work.
>
> Here's how to unwind it 👇🏽👇🏽

**Comments**

1/9 If there's pain involved, see a pelvic health physio. This is a real
speciality with real training, and the breathing below supports that work, it
doesn't replace it.

2/9 Step one. Learn what letting go feels like somewhere easy first. Unclench
your jaw. Drop your shoulders. Most women can't find the pelvic floor until
they've found the easy ones.

3/9 Step two. Breathe out longer than you breathe in. Four in, six or eight out.
The long out breath is the switch that takes your body off alert.

4/9 Step three. Put a hand on your lower belly and feel it move outward on the
in breath. If nothing moves down there, you're breathing in your chest, and a
chest breath keeps everything braced.

5/9 Step four. On each out breath, let go rather than squeeze. We've all been
told to do more clenching. If the problem is a floor that won't release, more
clenching makes it worse.

6/9 Step five. Practise five minutes a day, on the floor, knees up, nothing
sexual involved. You're teaching a new default, and defaults are built by
repetition, not intensity.

7/9 Step six. Then practise it in bed, clothed, with your partner's hand resting
on your belly. Same breathing, no expectation. She learns safe by evidence.

8/9 Step seven. Use the same breath when things do start. Long out breath at the
exact moment you notice the grip. That's the whole skill.

9/9 Step eight. Give it six weeks. Bodies unlearn bracing slowly, and the ones
that took years to learn it need more than a fortnight.

**Replies**

> "I've been doing pelvic floor squeezes for years. Was that wrong?"
>
> Not wrong, just the opposite prescription. Squeezes are for a floor that's too
> loose. If yours is too tight, release work comes first. A physio can tell you
> which you've got in one appointment.

> "How do I even know if I'm gripping?"
>
> Lie down and notice whether your belly moves on the in breath. If it doesn't,
> something's holding. That's your first honest answer.
